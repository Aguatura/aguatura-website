Project Location (Washington, D.C.)

On-Site Fieldwork: Rock Creek Park

During our North American field research in 2026, the Aguatura team visited Washington, D.C., to analyze best-practice models for urban watershed and forest management. Rock Creek Park is an extraordinary natural oasis running directly through the U.S. capital.

The Rock Creek Conservancy plays a pivotal role in protecting this unique ecosystem by uniting local community members, volunteers, and federal authorities into an active, long-term conservation effort.

Key Conservation Initiatives

Weed Warriors & Invasive Plant Removal

To combat aggressive invasive vines and plants that choke native trees and reduce forest canopy health, the Conservancy trains local volunteers through the "Weed Warriors" program. Certified leaders gain specialized botanical knowledge to supervise independent removal events across various park sections.

Stream Cleanups & Water Protection

Rock Creek flows directly into the Potomac River and Chesapeake Bay. Through annual large-scale cleanup events, thousands of community members systematically remove trash and urban waste from the creek beds, protecting aquatic ecosystems and generating critical pollution tracking data.

Stormwater Management & RainScapes

Heavy rain runoff washes urban pollutants and oil straight into natural waterways, causing severe soil erosion. The Conservancy promotes green infrastructure solutions, such as rain gardens, cisterns, and permeable surfaces, helping neighborhoods absorb stormwater naturally before it reaches the park.

Field Interview with Ryan McIntyre

Ryan McIntyre

Forest Resilience Manager, Rock Creek Conservancy

Former ecological researcher at the Smithsonian Institution. Currently oversees forest restoration, contractor teams, and community engagement throughout Rock Creek Park.

How do you describe your collaboration with the National Park Service (NPS)?
"We describe our partnership as being 'hand in glove'—basically moving together. Every year, we lay out an annual work plan for our volunteer projects in the park. Every Monday, I meet with park partners to establish synergies, set expectations, and ensure we share a singular vision for what's best for the park."
How do you ensure volunteers correctly identify and target invasive species?
"It's all about communication and structured training. At every event, we take 10 minutes to walk through plant identification. Furthermore, through our 'Weed Warriors' program, community members go through extensive training so they can lead groups or steward their local park sections independently."
"Our ultimate goal is to empower community members to take true ownership of local nature, so neighborhoods can steward their own forest sections."
— Ryan McIntyre, Forest Resilience Manager
What role does diversity and inclusion play in your urban conservation efforts?
"We want to serve the whole community, not just wealthy neighborhoods. We intentionally direct grant funding into historically underserved areas, run summer youth programs, and build new volunteer networks where people don't know us yet."
How do you measure success and handle challenges like climate change?
"We track clear proxies: square footage of invasive plants removed, number of trees freed from choking vines to preserve the canopy, and volunteer engagement hours. To tackle climate change, we diversify funding and collaborate with regional networks like the Capital Area Natural Resources Management group to share adaptive strategies."

Aguatura Takeaways for Global Replication

Consistency Builds Trust

Holding regular, scheduled monthly events creates reliable community habits, even if initial turnout is small.

The Multiplier Model

Training local 'Weed Warriors' allows conservation efforts to scale organically without requiring staff oversight at every site.

Clear Agency Alignment

Structured weekly and annual planning prevents administrative friction between non-profits and public authorities.
