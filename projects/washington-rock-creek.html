Main Challenges

Urban natural ecosystems face an unprecedented combination of environmental pressures and intensive urban development.

  • Invasive Species Pressure — Non-native vines choke mature tree canopies, preventing natural forest regeneration.
  • Stormwater Runoff & Erosion — Heavy rainwater washes off concrete surfaces, carrying urban pollutants directly into the creek.
  • Urban Pressure & Fragmented Habitat — Surrounding dense infrastructure isolates wildlife populations and strains public park resources.

Approach Rock Creek Conservancy

Steps away from the U.S. Capitol lies Rock Creek Park, a 710-hectare (7.1 km²) natural oasis forming Washington, D.C.'s green lung. Rock Creek Conservancy leads its protection by combining community action, ecological restoration, and strong institutional partnerships.

Weed Warriors & Plant Removal

Training local volunteers through the "Weed Warriors" program to independently identify and safely remove invasive vines choking mature trees.

Stream Cleanups & Protection

Organizing large-scale community cleanups along tributary paths flowing into the Potomac River to remove urban trash and protect aquatic life.

Stormwater & RainScapes

Promoting green infrastructure solutions, such as rain gardens and permeable surfaces, helping neighborhoods absorb stormwater naturally.

Expert Insights

Ryan McIntyre

Forest Resilience Manager, Rock Creek Conservancy

Leads participatory ecosystem management, volunteer capacity-building, and forest restoration strategies across Rock Creek Park.

Protecting an urban forest requires more than ecological knowledge—it demands deep community integration, institutional synergy, and grassroots empowerment.

How does your partnership with federal and local authorities work?

"Our collaboration with the National Park Service is truly hand-in-glove. We plan work together season by season and meet weekly to coordinate projects and establish synergies."

How do you mobilize and train community "Weed Warriors"?

We rely on regular volunteer events, peer outreach, and structured 10-minute field briefings at every session to ensure high-quality restoration work on the ground.

How do you integrate Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion into your projects?

"Conservation belongs to everyone. We intentionally focus funding and new projects on historically underfunded communities rather than only wealthy neighborhoods."

What metrics do you use to evaluate ecological progress?

Progress is measured through total engaged volunteer hours, square footage of invasive plants cleared, and the number of tree-strangling vines cut from mature canopies.

What are the main future challenges regarding climate change and funding?

Selecting resilient tree species for future climates while diversifying revenue streams to remain independent of public budget shifts.

"My goal is to empower local neighborhood groups to take full ownership of their nearby park sections and lead restoration autonomously."

How can you take action

Micro Level

  • Participate in local Weed Warriors events or neighborhood park cleanups.
  • Install a rain garden or rain barrel on your residential property to capture runoff.
  • Learn to identify non-native invasive vines in your yard to stop their spread.

Meso Level

  • Form neighborhood stewardship groups to adopt and care for local park sections.
  • Partner with municipal authorities to align community volunteer efforts with formal plans.
  • Organize youth environmental education workshops in urban communities.
