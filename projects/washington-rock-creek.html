Main Challenges

Urban natural ecosystems face an unprecedented combination of environmental pressures:

  • Invasive Species Pressure — Non-native vines choke mature tree canopies, preventing natural forest regeneration and killing native flora.
  • Stormwater Runoff & Erosion — Heavy rainwater washes off concrete, carrying urban pollutants directly into the creek and causing severe bank erosion.
  • Urban Pressure & Fragmented Habitat — Surrounding dense infrastructure isolates wildlife populations and strains public park resources.

Approach Rockcreek Conservancy

Just steps away from the political institutions of the U.S. capital winds Rock Creek Park, a 710-hectare (7.1 km²) natural sanctuary described as the green spine of Washington, D.C. Behind the preservation of this urban green lung is Rock Creek Conservancy, a non-profit organization that relies primarily on human connection, grassroots action, and strong institutional partnerships to protect local biodiversity.

Weed Warriors & Invasive Plant Removal

To combat aggressive invasive vines and plants that choke native trees, the Conservancy trains local volunteers through the "Weed Warriors" program. Certified leaders gain specialized botanical knowledge to supervise independent removal events across various park sections.

Stream Cleanups & Water Protection

Rock Creek flows directly into the Potomac River and Chesapeake Bay. Through annual large-scale cleanup events, thousands of community members systematically remove trash and urban waste from the creek beds, protecting aquatic ecosystems and generating critical pollution tracking data.

Stormwater Management & RainScapes

Heavy rain runoff washes urban pollutants and oil straight into natural waterways, causing severe soil erosion. The Conservancy promotes green infrastructure solutions, such as rain gardens, cisterns, and permeable surfaces, helping neighborhoods absorb stormwater naturally before it reaches the park.

Experts Insights: Reinventing Urban Conservation Through Community

Ryan McIntyre

Forest Resilience Manager, Rock Creek Conservancy

Leads participatory ecosystem management, volunteer capacity-building, and forest restoration strategies across Rock Creek Park in Washington, D.C.

How does your partnership with federal and local authorities work?
"We describe our collaboration with the National Park Service as being 'hand and glove,' basically moving together. Every year, we build a joint work plan where we lay out season by season what we're going to be doing, and we have weekly meetings with our counterparts at the park to determine what we're going to focus on and establish synergies."
How do you mobilize and train community "Weed Warriors"?
The Conservancy relies on predictable events for neighborhood ownership, a network effect where dedicated volunteers train their social circles, and structured 10-minute field briefings at every session to ensure high-quality restoration work on the ground.
How do you integrate Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion into your projects?
"We don't want to be only working in really wealthy neighborhoods. We want to be serving the whole community. Whenever we are picking out new projects or using government grants, we are usually intentionally looking to serve underfunded areas."
What metrics do you use to evaluate ecological progress?
Progress is measured through three main quantitative indicators: total engaged volunteer hours, square footage of invasive plants cleared, and the number of vines cut from mature trees to save the forest canopy.
What are the main future challenges regarding climate change and funding?
To adapt to climate change, the team researches which tree species will withstand changing weather patterns in D.C. To maintain financial resilience against fluctuating public budgets, the Conservancy diversifies revenue through individual gifts, major donations, and targeted grants.
"I'm hoping to help us expand and growing our volunteer base into smaller little pocket communities—people who are dedicated to a specific park area—and take on more projects that can be led by the community itself."
— Ryan McIntyre, Forest Resilience Manager

How can you take action

Micro Level

  • Participate in local Weed Warriors events or neighborhood park cleanups.
  • Install a rain garden or permeable surfaces on your residential property to capture runoff.
  • Learn to identify non-native invasive vines in your own yard to stop their spread into public forests.

Meso Level

  • Form neighborhood stewardship groups to adopt and care for local park sections.
  • Partner with municipal authorities to align community volunteer efforts with formal management plans.
  • Organize youth environmental education workshops in underserved urban communities.
