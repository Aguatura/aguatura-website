Main Challenges

South Florida's subtropical ecosystems face severe risks from rapid urban growth, habitat loss, and climate pressures.

  • Rapid loss of native tropical plants due to invasive species and urban sprawl.
  • Sea-level rise and saltwater intrusion threatening freshwater and wetlands.
  • More frequent extreme weather events and hurricanes impacting collections.
  • Erosion and changing water cycles affecting regional watershed stability.
  • Need for stronger public engagement and science education for biodiversity.

Fairchild Approach

Combining the classic activities of botanical gardens with new ideas, multidisciplinary approaches, and cross-sector partnerships—ranging from NASA space science and urban restoration to art collaborations and community education—Fairchild leads modern biodiversity preservation.

NASA Partnership: Botanical Labs & Space Agriculture

In a groundbreaking collaboration with NASA's Kennedy Space Center, Fairchild operates specialized botany labs to identify and evaluate edible plant species suitable for space exploration. Through the "Growing Beyond Earth" project, middle and high school students test hundreds of crop varieties in custom chambers (Veggie & Advanced Plant Habitat units). Using tissue culture micropropagation, automated environmental controls, and 360-degree gravity simulators, researchers study plant growth dynamics, stress tolerance, and microgravity responses to provide essential nutrition and psychological benefits for future long-duration missions to the Moon and Mars.

The Million Orchid Project: Urban Restoration & Citizen Science

The Million Orchid Project is a landmark urban ecology restoration initiative designed to re-establish millions of native orchid species into South Florida's urban landscape. Historically over-collected to near-extinction, native orchids are micropropagated in sterile laboratory environments. Through partnerships with local schools and community organizations, students learn sterile tissue culture techniques, germinating microscopic seeds in specialized media flasks before transplanting young seedlings onto urban canopy trees. This initiative restores key mycorrhizal fungal associations and natural insect pollination networks while empowering local communities as active environmental stewards.

Expert Insights

Dr. Jason Downing

Director of Research, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden

Leading research on micropropagation, citizen science, and urban ecosystem restoration in Miami.

At Fairchild, conservation is no longer confined to scientists and laboratories. Through initiatives like the Million Orchid Project, researchers are combining science, education, and citizen participation to restore endangered species while transforming public engagement.

Citizen Science & Public Engagement

By encouraging residents, schools, and volunteers to observe and monitor orchids planted across South Florida, researchers gain valuable data while fostering a deeper connection to nature.

"We cannot monitor every orchid ourselves, but thousands of residents can. The community becomes our eyes on the ground."

Combating Poaching Through Abundance

Increasing the legal availability of native orchids dramatically reduces illegal collection pressure in fragile wild habitats.

"If we grow a million orchids, we destroy the black market for that species. There is no reason to poach a plant from the wild when anyone can obtain one legally."

Education as Conservation

Over 6,000 students actively participate in planting, tissue culture, and data collection, placing youth empowerment at the core of biodiversity protection.

"We have more than 6,000 students involved in the project. Education is not a side benefit of conservation. Education is conservation."

Inspiring Action Through Beauty

The visual appeal of orchids serves as an engaging entry point to spark environmental awareness among diverse communities.

"People may not care about conservation at first, but they care about beauty. You can lead with beautification and end up achieving conservation."

A Continuous Process of Restoration

In an era marked by climate change and urban expansion, ecological restoration requires ongoing adaptation rather than one-time fixes.

"Restoration is not a destination. Restoration is a process that never truly ends. When people feel ownership of nature, restoration moves from being a project to becoming a movement."

How to Take Action

Micro Level

  • Plant native species and pollinator-friendly flowers in your garden or balcony.
  • Support and visit botanical gardens to experience and champion plant conservation firsthand.
  • Conserve water and reduce chemical fertilizers around your home.

Meso Level

  • Join citizen science programs like the Million Orchid Project.
  • Partner with local conservation groups and community gardens.
  • Advocate for urban tree canopy protection and sustainable policies.
