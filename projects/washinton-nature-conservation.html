Main Challenges

The Washington D.C. capital region faces profound environmental hurdles driven by rapid urbanization, extensive surface sealing, and climate change pressures. High levels of impervious surfaces lead to severe stormwater runoff, threatening local urban watersheds, water quality in the Potomac and Anacostia river basins, and aquatic habitats.

Furthermore, habitat fragmentation, the proliferation of invasive species across urban green spaces, and urban heat island effects threaten urban biodiversity and ecosystem resilience. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated grassroots conservation, active stream restoration, and robust community engagement across all neighborhoods.

Nature Forward Approach

Inclusive Community Engagement

Lowering financial and social barriers to membership. Transitioning from traditional top-down models to active listening partnerships with diverse local communities and neighborhood hubs across the Capital Region.

Watershed & Stream Restoration

Managing stormwater runoff and restoring urban watersheds at Woodend Sanctuary and regional parks. Transforming technical engineering projects into hands-on educational opportunities for youth and volunteers.

Local Advocacy & Accountability

Pushing local county and state levels of government to enforce environmental regulations. Collaborating in strong coalitions to protect natural habitats against unchecked urban development and policy rollbacks.

Expert Insights

Alison Pearce (Nature Forward): “There Is No Single Solution, Only Collective Strategies for Change”
In a wide-ranging interview on conservation, community engagement, environmental education, and organizational leadership, Alison Pearce, Executive Director of Nature Forward, shared her vision for creating lasting environmental impact. Rather than searching for quick fixes, she advocates for long-term strategies centered on people, partnerships, and community empowerment.

Fighting Invasive Species Is a Long-Term Commitment

One of the central themes of the discussion was the challenge of managing invasive species. Alison Pearce stressed that ecological restoration is often misunderstood as a one-time intervention.

“When people ask about the solution to invasive plants or environmental challenges, I often say there isn’t a single solution. There are many strategies that, together, can make a difference.”

She explained that many people expect immediate results after removing invasive species, only to be disappointed when they return.

“You remove invasive plants one year, and they come back the next. That doesn’t mean the work failed. Nature operates on long timescales. Restoration requires persistence and continuous management.”

For the CEO of Nature Forward, success is not defined by a permanent victory over nature’s challenges but by sustained progress over time.

Balancing Ambition and Focus

As an organization, Nature Forward operates across multiple areas, including environmental education, habitat restoration, conservation advocacy, community engagement, and volunteer programs.

While this diversity is a strength, it also creates challenges.

“Ideas are never the problem. The real challenge is filtering them, prioritizing them, and sometimes deciding what not to do.”

The leadership described a constant flow of suggestions coming from staff, members, volunteers, and community partners. The difficult part is identifying which initiatives align most closely with the organization’s mission and available resources.

“There will always be a million ideas. The hard work is deciding which ones can have the greatest impact.”

Education as the Foundation of Conservation

Throughout the interview, she repeatedly returned to the importance of education.

Whether restoring wetlands, protecting forests, or advocating for policy change, she sees education as the common thread connecting all of Nature Forward’s work.

“At the end of the day, almost everything we do is education. Even restoration projects are really about building understanding, skills, and capacity within communities.”

She emphasized that environmental education goes far beyond classroom instruction. It involves helping people understand their local ecosystems and empowering them to take action.

Children, in particular, play a critical role.

“When young people have positive experiences in nature, those experiences often become part of their identity for the rest of their lives.”

Yet Nature Forward’s strategy extends beyond direct engagement with children. The organization also invests in teachers, multiplying its impact across thousands of students.

“If you train one teacher, that teacher may influence hundreds of students over the course of a career.”

Reaching Beyond Traditional Audiences

The executive director believes that conservation organizations must move beyond serving only those who are already interested in nature.

“We won’t succeed because we found the perfect solution. We’ll succeed because millions of people adopt better practices.”

She illustrated this point with the example of native plants. While ecologists may identify ideal species for biodiversity, widespread adoption depends on accessibility.

“If people can’t find a plant in a local garden center and have to grow it themselves from seeds collected on the roadside, that’s not a strategy that can scale.”

For the CEO of Nature Forward, practical adoption is often more important than theoretical perfection.

Redefining Humanity’s Relationship with Nature

Another important theme was the need to rethink who belongs in conservation.

She rejects the notion that some people are inherently “nature people” while others are not.

“Everyone has a relationship with nature. Their experience may simply look different from someone else’s.”

Nature Forward seeks to build on these existing experiences rather than expecting people to conform to a traditional environmental identity.

This philosophy is especially important when engaging communities that have historically been underrepresented in environmental spaces.

Partnerships Beyond the Environmental Sector

The leadership highlighted several partnerships that demonstrate how conservation can intersect with social and economic goals.

One example involves workforce development programs that provide environmental training to people seeking employment. Participants learn skills such as ecological landscaping and stormwater management while improving their job prospects.

“Some of our most impactful partnerships are not with environmental organizations at all.”

These collaborations help embed conservation within broader community priorities such as employment, public health, and neighborhood improvement.

An Organization That Continues to Evolve

Nature Forward, formerly known as the Audubon Naturalist Society, has undergone significant changes throughout its history.

The organization recently rebranded to better reflect its evolving mission and commitment to inclusion.

Alison Pearce explained that the goal is to create pathways for participation that are accessible to everyone, regardless of background, income, or prior experience with nature.

The organization has also rethought its membership model, making engagement easier and more welcoming for community members who contribute through programs, workshops, and volunteer activities.

“The Purpose Is Not the Organization”

Perhaps the most powerful message of the interview concerned organizational purpose.

She warned against nonprofits becoming overly focused on their own survival or growth.

“The purpose of the organization is not the organization. The purpose is the change we want to create in the world.”

This mindset shapes how Nature Forward collaborates with other institutions, government agencies, community groups, and advocacy organizations.

Success, she argues, should always be measured by progress toward environmental and social outcomes rather than organizational prestige.

Investing in People to Create Lasting Change

As the interview concluded, she returned to what she sees as the organization’s ultimate mission: investing in people.

“We invest in people, in their skills, in their motivation, and in their ability to care for the places where they live.”

Whether through environmental education, volunteer programs, restoration projects, or community partnerships, Nature Forward’s approach is rooted in empowerment.

For the CEO of Nature Forward, meaningful environmental progress does not come from a single breakthrough solution. It comes from giving people the knowledge, confidence, and opportunities to become active stewards of their communities.

In a world searching for quick fixes, Nature Forward offers a different vision: one built on patience, participation, education, and the collective power of people working together for nature.

How can you take action

Micro Level

  • Support local cities/NGOs in your neighborhood.
  • Plant native flora in your garden or balcony and create simple rain gardens to reduce stormwater runoff.
  • Participate in local citizen science and neighborhood clean-ups to protect urban waterways.

Meso Level

  • Partner with Neighborhood Hubs: Collaborate with local community centers and grassroots organizations to listen to local priorities before launching programs.
  • Advocate at Local Government Levels: Attend city council or county meetings to hold decision-makers accountable for watershed protection laws.
  • Foster Inclusive Membership: Modernize organizational structures by removing high financial paywalls and embracing diverse community leaders.
